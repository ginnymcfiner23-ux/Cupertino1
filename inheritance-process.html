How the Cupertino house passes by trust

Assuming the house is held in a revocable living trust (trustee: [TRUSTEE NAME], trust: [TRUST NAME]), the transfer at the grantor's death is handled through trust administration, not probate court — keeping it private and usually faster. The steps below are the standard California process; the trust document itself and California Probate Code govern the actual timeline and duties.

Not legal advice

This is a general checklist. Statutes change (see CA Legislative Information and CA Courts). A successor trustee can be personally liable for mistakes — consult a California estate attorney and CPA before acting.

Step-by-step checklist

Step 1 — Secure documents and property (immediately after death)

  • Order multiple certified death certificates (needed for banks, title companies, insurers, county recorder).
  • Locate the trust document and all amendments, the pour-over will, and any property deed.
  • Identify the successor trustee ([SUCCESSOR TRUSTEE NAME]) and all beneficiaries.
  • Secure the property: keep homeowners insurance active, check utilities, arrange maintenance.

Source: general California trust administration practice; see CA Courts self-help and trust administration guidance (e.g., Probate Code § 16060 — trustee duty to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed).

Step 2 — Statutory notice to beneficiaries and heirs (within 60 days)

  • When the revocable trust becomes irrevocable on the grantor's death, the trustee must send a statutory notice to all trust beneficiaries and all of the decedent's legal heirs (including heirs who receive nothing) — California Probate Code § 16061.7.
  • Deadline: generally within 60 days of the event making the trust irrevocable.
  • Effect: starts a 120-day window during which the trust can be contested. Defective notice leaves the trust open to challenge indefinitely.

Source: Cal. Prob. Code § 16061.7 — leginfo.legislature.ca.gov.

Step 3 — Tax identification, bank account, and asset inventory

  • Apply for a federal EIN for the trust (it becomes irrevocable at death).
  • Open a trust bank account; never commingle trust funds with personal funds.
  • Inventory all trust assets (house, accounts, investments, the inherited cash sum) and non-trust assets.
  • Assets held outside the trust may require a separate probate proceeding or a Heggstad petition — confirm with an attorney.

Step 4 — Debts, expenses, and tax filings

  • Pay the decedent's final bills, debts, and administration expenses from trust assets before any distribution.
  • File the decedent's final personal income tax return (Form 1040) and, if required, California Form 540.
  • File trust income tax returns (federal Form 1041 / California Form 541) if the trust earns income.
  • If the total estate exceeds the federal estate tax exemption ($15,000,000 per person in 2026), file federal Form 706 within 9 months of death (portability election for spouses requires a timely return). California has no estate or inheritance tax.

Sources: IRS.gov · Estate tax · CA FTB · Gifts and inheritance.

Step 5 — Transfer title of the house (real property)

  • Record an Affidavit of Death of Trustee with a certified death certificate at the Santa Clara County Recorder to vest title in the successor trustee (or in the heir, per the trust terms).
  • File a Preliminary Change in Ownership Report (PCOR) with the county recorder when recording the deed-related documents; the Assessor uses it to determine whether reassessment is required.
  • Under Prop 13, reassessment of inherited property occurs as of the date of death — not the distribution date.

Sources: Santa Clara County Assessor (2026 Assessment Information booklet: PCOR requirement; inheritance reassessed as of date of death) · CA BOE · Proposition 19.

Step 6 — Establish tax basis (step-up)

  • Obtain a date-of-death appraisal (fair market value at death) — this becomes the heir's cost basis under IRS step-up rules (IRC § 1014) and determines any future capital gains tax on sale.
  • If the estate files Form 706 and issues Schedule A (Form 8971), use the same reported value (consistent basis reporting).

Sources: IRS Pub 551 · IRS Pub 559.

Step 7 — Property tax exclusion claim (if keeping the house)

  • If an heir will use the home as their primary residence: file the homeowner's exemption (BOE-266) within 1 year of transfer, and the Claim for Reassessment Exclusion for Transfer Between Parent and Child (BOE-19-P) within 3 years of transfer (or before transfer to a third party, whichever is earlier), with the Santa Clara County Assessor.
  • Value cap: exclusion protects assessed value + $1M (biennially adjusted). Market value far above that → partial reassessment.
  • If the home is kept as a rental or second home: no exclusion — full reassessment to market value.

Sources: CA BOE · Proposition 19 · BOE LTA 2026/026 · SCC Assessor claim forms.

Step 8 — Distribute assets, account, and close

  • After debts, taxes, and expenses are paid and the 120-day contest period has run, distribute the house and the inherited cash sum according to the trust terms.
  • Provide a written accounting to beneficiaries; obtain signed receipts/releases; then terminate the trust.

Timing: simple trust administrations often take roughly 6–18 months; disputes or tax filings can take longer (illustrative only, not a guarantee).

If there is no trust (or the house was never transferred into it)

  • The house may need to go through California probate before title can transfer — slower, public, and with court fees.
  • Small-estate procedures exist below certain thresholds (California Prob. Code § 13100 family allowance/small-estate affidavit rules — thresholds change; verify current figures).
  • A Heggstad petition can sometimes confirm a house belongs to a trust even if the deed was never formally transferred.

Source: CA Courts probate self-help · Cal. Prob. Code via leginfo.legislature.ca.gov.
