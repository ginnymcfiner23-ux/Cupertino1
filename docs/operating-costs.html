Rental economics — the property tax does the damage, not the operating costs

Net operating income

The green line assumes the assessed value survives. The dashed line is what happens if the building is reassessed to market value at the inheritance — the likely outcome, because a building with two tenancies is not the transferor’s principal residence.

Reading the two lines

The operating costs of the building — water, sewer, stormwater, electricity, insurance, maintenance — are roughly $41,000 a year in 2026 and they are almost entirely under the owner’s control. The property tax swing of roughly $39,000 a year is not under the owner’s control at all: it is determined by a statute, a court and the drafting of a trust.

That asymmetry is the argument for selling PROPERTY_SF after the inheritance if the base is not protected: the owner can control the operating costs and the sale costs, but not the reassessment.

Insurance — the largest blank in the model

No address-specific premium is published by any official source

There is no official or published price for a homeowners (HO-3) policy on PROPERTY_CUPERTINO or a landlord (DP-3) policy on PROPERTY_SF. Insurance premiums are set by the carrier, the risk, the deductible and the replacement cost, and they are not published anywhere. The official column on this site is therefore blank by rule, and the model carries an assumption band instead: $1,500–$5,000 a year for the Cupertino house and $1,500–$4,000 a year for the San Francisco building, with the owner’s $1,500 estimate at the very bottom of both bands.

Two things make this more than a rounding error. First, the California FAIR Plan — the state’s insurer of last resort — is the fallback if an admitted carrier declines, and its terms were also not confirmed from an official page during this build (flag F9). Second, a landlord policy and a homeowner policy are different products: the San Francisco building needs a landlord form (DP-3), which is priced differently from an HO-3.

Action: obtain written quotes from at least two admitted carriers for each property, and ask each of them directly what they would do if they declined — and what the FAIR Plan would cost instead.
