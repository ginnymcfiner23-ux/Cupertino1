Bottom line (illustrative, not advice)

1. Sell the Cupertino house shortly after inheritance

The step-up in basis wipes out built-in capital gains, so a sale near the date of death generates little or no capital gains tax. Net proceeds can be reinvested in a diversified portfolio (stocks, savings, etc.).

Why: under Prop 19, keeping the Cupertino house as a rental triggers full reassessment to market value; even moving in as primary residence only caps the break at assessed value + ~$1M. With an assessed value around $550K and a market value around $3.1M (and growing), the property tax jump is large — roughly $24,000+/year today at 2026 values, and much more by 2040–2050. Rental income is unlikely to cover tax + insurance + maintenance.

2. Keep the San Francisco home as primary residence + rental income

The SF house carries a very low assessed value (≈ $114,248 per owner records → ≈ $2,439/year tax) and two units renting at ≈ $7,150/month. The owner can live in the third unit without losing rental income — a strong positive cash-flow position.

Moving to Cupertino would give up this arrangement and trigger a new Prop 19 assessment on the Cupertino home. Keeping the SF home as the primary residence appears financially preferable unless a move to Cupertino is needed for family reasons.

Inherited cash may exceed the federal estate tax threshold

The inheritance also includes a considerable sum of money that could exceed the federal estate tax exemption ($15,000,000 per person in 2026, indexed after that). Estates above the exemption owe federal estate tax (top rate 40%), paid by the estate before distribution — heirs do not pay inheritance tax, and California imposes none. Form 706 is due 9 months after death (portability election for married couples). See Inheritance & estate taxes.
